**Q1. Select ALL that apply: A PetVax analyst studies only completed appointments to understand why appointments are missed. Which biases or fallacies from Chapter 13 does this approach risk?**

*Short description: Tests your ability to diagnose multiple overlapping analytical biases when sample selection distorts an investigation.*

A. Survivorship bias — analysis focuses on visible cases while ignoring missed appointments ← ✓ CORRECT
*Feedback: Correct — Section 13.6 defines survivorship bias as focusing on cases that remain visible after a selection process while ignoring cases that disappeared, failed, or were never observed. Studying only completed appointments to understand missed ones is a textbook example.*

B. Simpson's paradox — the aggregate data reverses within groups
*Feedback: Incorrect — Simpson's paradox involves aggregate-subgroup reversal, not sample-selection issues. The scenario does not describe aggregate vs. subgroup comparison.*

C. Selection bias — observed cases differ from the population of interest ← ✓ CORRECT
*Feedback: Correct — Section 13.7 lists selection bias: "Observed cases differ from the population of interest." The population of interest is all appointments; the analyst observes only completed ones.*

D. Goodhart's Law — the metric becomes the target
*Feedback: Incorrect — Goodhart's Law involves metric-target distortion, not sample selection. No metric is being used as a target in this scenario.*

E. Missing-data bias — missingness may be related to the outcome ← ✓ CORRECT
*Feedback: Correct — Section 13.7 lists missing-data bias: "Missingness is related to the outcome." Missed appointments may be systematically different from completed ones in ways that matter for the analysis.*

**Hint:** Think about which cases the analyst can see and which cases are systematically absent from the dataset.

**Explanation:** Section 13.6 defines survivorship bias: "analysis focuses on cases that remain visible after a selection process while ignoring cases that disappeared, failed, withdrew, or were never observed." Section 13.7 adds selection bias ("observed cases differ from the population of interest") and missing-data bias ("missingness is related to the outcome"). Together, these three concepts explain why studying only completed appointments to understand missed appointments produces unreliable conclusions. Simpson's paradox (aggregate-subgroup reversal) and Goodhart's Law (metric-target distortion) are different failure modes not present in this scenario.

**Q3. A call center rewards employees for reducing average call time. Call time drops, but repeat-call rates rise and satisfaction falls. Which concept from Section 13.6 does this scenario best illustrate?**

*Short description: Tests your ability to recognize Goodhart's Law — when targeting a metric changes behavior at the expense of the real goal — in a classic workplace scenario.*

A. Simpson's paradox
*Feedback: Incorrect — Simpson's paradox involves aggregate vs. subgroup reversal, not metric-target distortion.*

B. Survivorship bias
*Feedback: Incorrect — Survivorship bias involves focusing on visible cases while ignoring missing ones.*

C. Goodhart's Law
*Feedback: Correct — Section 13.6 defines Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, people change their behavior to improve the measure, sometimes at the expense of the real goal." The call-center example is the chapter's canonical illustration.*

D. The McNamara fallacy
*Feedback: Incorrect — The McNamara fallacy involves relying on what is easy to quantify while ignoring important unquantifiable factors. The call-time scenario is about metric-target distortion, not about ignoring unquantifiable factors.*

**Hint:** The key is what happens when a measure becomes a target — the chapter has a named law for this.

**Explanation:** Section 13.6 defines Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, people change their behavior to improve the measure, sometimes at the expense of the real goal." The chapter uses a call-center example nearly identical to this scenario, where employees rush conversations to meet the call-time target, causing unresolved problems, repeat calls, and lower satisfaction. This is metric-target distortion — the measured number improves while the real goal (service quality) worsens. The McNamara fallacy (ignoring unquantifiable factors) and Simpson's paradox (aggregate-subgroup reversal) describe different failure modes.

**Q4. A manager notices that Program A has a higher overall success rate (81.8%) than Program B (80.9%). However, when students are grouped by risk level, Program B performs better in both the lower-risk and higher-risk groups. Which concept does this illustrate?**

*Short description: Tests your ability to recognize Simpson's paradox — when aggregated data reverses direction within meaningful subgroups.*

A. Goodhart's Law
*Feedback: Incorrect — Goodhart's Law involves metric-target distortion, not subgroup reversal.*

B. Simpson's paradox
*Feedback: Correct — Section 13.6 defines Simpson's paradox: "A relationship in aggregated data changes or reverses after the data is divided into meaningful groups." The chapter uses a nearly identical numerical example with support programs.*

C. The McNamara fallacy
*Feedback: Incorrect — The McNamara fallacy involves ignoring unquantifiable factors, not aggregate-subgroup reversal.*

D. Survivorship bias
*Feedback: Incorrect — Survivorship bias involves focusing on visible cases while ignoring missing ones.*

**Hint:** The chapter provides a numerical table with two risk groups and two programs — look for the paradox where the overall winner loses in every subgroup.

**Explanation:** Section 13.6 defines Simpson's paradox: "A relationship in aggregated data changes or reverses after the data is divided into meaningful groups." The chapter includes a worked example with two support programs evaluated across lower-risk and higher-risk students, where Program B outperforms Program A in both subgroups but Program A has a slightly higher overall rate because it served mostly lower-risk students. The arithmetic is correct but the aggregate comparison is misleading. This is fundamentally different from Goodhart's Law (metric-target distortion), the McNamara fallacy (ignoring unquantifiable factors), or survivorship bias (missing-case blindness).
